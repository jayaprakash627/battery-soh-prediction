How much battery is left,
without draining it?

To measure what a battery really holds, you have to run it flat and count. That takes hours, and the bike is off the road the whole time. But every battery gets charged, and a charger already measures voltage, current and temperature. This reads a few minutes of a normal charge and works out how much life is left — knowing nothing about the battery's past.

Why it matters. The normal way to check a battery is a full drain-and-refill test: about 4 hours per battery, and the bike earns nothing while it sits there. This uses a charge that was going to happen anyway. For a 150-bike fleet that is roughly 600 hours of downtime a year that you get back.

The score depends on how you test it

Same model, same batteries. The only thing that changes is which data is hidden from it during testing. The gap between these three numbers is the real finding here.

Why the first number is misleading

Shuffling means charge number 100 is used for training and charge 101 for testing. Those two are the same battery an hour apart — almost the same reading. The model scores well by copying a neighbour it has already seen. That answers "can you fill a gap in a test you already watched", which nobody needs.

Hiding a whole battery asks the real question: here is one you have never met, how healthy is it? That is the number reported everywhere on this page, and the number the model was chosen on.

Where it stops

  • —

Left out on purpose

  • How many times it has been charged. This is the strongest clue in the lab and useless in real life. Health drops as the count rises, so the model would just be reading the lab's schedule. A second-hand battery does not arrive with its count written on it.
  • How much it held last time. Same problem, worse — that is the very thing we are trying to work out.
  • Which battery it is. Eight batteries is not enough to learn a per-battery adjustment that would carry over. It is exactly enough to memorise these eight.
